What Is an Indemnification Clause?

An indemnification clause requires one party to cover the other party's losses if certain bad things happen. Whether that obligation is mutual, one-sided, capped, or uncapped is one of the most consequential decisions in any contract.

What it does

Indemnification means one party agrees to compensate the other party for specific losses or claims. In a typical software contract, the vendor might indemnify the customer if the vendor's product infringes a third party's intellectual property; the customer might indemnify the vendor if the customer misuses the product in ways that cause third-party harm.

The clause has two basic flavors. Mutual indemnification means both parties indemnify each other for claims arising from their own actions. One-sided indemnification means only one party (usually the customer) takes on indemnification obligations. The vendor's reciprocal obligations are narrow or nonexistent.

The interaction between indemnification and other clauses matters enormously. An indemnification obligation can blow past the contract's limitation-of-liability cap unless the cap explicitly applies to indemnification. So a contract with a $10,000 liability cap and an uncapped indemnification clause has, effectively, no liability cap at all.

Why it matters

One-sided indemnification turns the customer into the vendor's insurance policy. If a vendor employee leaks customer data and a third party sues both companies, a one-sided indemnification clause says the customer pays the vendor's legal fees. That's not just unfair — it's actively dangerous. These clauses tend to trigger in situations nobody pictured at signing. The classic scenario: a vendor's security failure leads to a regulatory investigation, the regulator deposes both parties, and the customer ends up paying tens of thousands in legal fees defending the vendor's response. The vendor walks away clean. The customer foots the bill.

The language to watch for

Here's the kind of language you'll see in real contracts:

Customer shall indemnify, defend, and hold harmless Provider and its affiliates from and against any and all claims, damages, losses, costs, and expenses (including reasonable attorneys' fees) arising out of or relating to Customer's use of the Service, except to the extent caused by Provider's gross negligence or willful misconduct.

What to redline

  • Make the indemnification mutual — both parties indemnify the other for claims arising from their own actions. This is the single biggest issue.
  • Add a "gross negligence and willful misconduct" carve-out for the indemnifying party. The customer shouldn't indemnify the vendor for the vendor's own bad behavior.
  • Cap indemnification obligations at the limitation-of-liability amount. Otherwise indemnification becomes an end-run around the liability cap.
  • Require the vendor to indemnify the customer for third-party IP claims that the vendor's product infringes. This is one place where vendor indemnification is industry-standard.
  • Require the indemnified party to give prompt notice of claims, allow the indemnifying party to control the defense, and not settle without consent. These procedural protections are routine.

What's market

Mutual indemnification is the norm in modern B2B contracts. IP indemnification from the vendor for their own product is standard. The carve-outs are sometimes negotiated. Pure one-way indemnification favoring the vendor is increasingly viewed as predatory drafting and shouldn't be accepted in any contract you'd consider an arms-length deal.
