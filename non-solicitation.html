What Is a Non-Solicitation Clause?

A non-solicitation clause prevents one party from poaching the other's employees, contractors, or customers for a specified period. Modern drafting often expands these clauses far beyond their original purpose.

What it does

A non-solicitation clause is a contractual restriction preventing one party from soliciting, recruiting, or hiring people associated with the other party. They originally existed to prevent customers from poaching the vendor's employees during or shortly after a service relationship — a reasonable concern when a vendor has invested heavily in training people who then go work directly for the customer.

Modern drafting has expanded these clauses dramatically. Today's non-solicitation language often captures not just direct employees but contractors, agents, and even "individuals who interacted with" the vendor. The duration has stretched from 6 months to 12, 18, or even 24 months. The geographic scope has expanded from local markets to worldwide.

These broader clauses are increasingly found unenforceable in court — but few customers want to test that theory in litigation. The clause does its work by deterrent effect: most customers will avoid hiring anyone who could conceivably trigger the restriction, even if the trigger would be legally questionable.

Why it matters

Non-solicitation clauses originally existed to protect vendors from a real harm: customers using the vendor's training and onboarding investment, then hiring those same employees away. That problem still exists. But modern drafting has weaponized the clause far beyond the original purpose. Read literally, some would prevent a customer from hiring a freelancer the vendor used three years ago, or from contracting with another company whose founder once sat in on a sales call. The customer rarely intends to violate the clause; they just don't have time to map every potential hire against the vendor's entire orbit before extending an offer.

The language to watch for

Here's the kind of language you'll see in real contracts:

During the term of this Agreement and for a period of twenty-four (24) months thereafter, Customer shall not, directly or indirectly, solicit, recruit, or hire any employee, contractor, agent, or other individual who has interacted with Provider in connection with the Services.

What to redline

  • Restrict the clause to direct employees of the vendor, not "individuals who interacted with" the vendor. The expansion language is the biggest single problem.
  • Reduce the duration to 12 months at most, ideally 6. Two-year non-solicits are increasingly viewed as overreach.
  • Add an exception for general solicitation — public job postings on LinkedIn, recruitment ads, and so on. These shouldn't trigger the clause.
  • Add an exception for individuals who approach the customer first, unsolicited. The clause should restrict active recruitment, not normal labor-market activity.
  • If the vendor is acquired or substantially restructures, the non-solicit terminates. Otherwise the clause persists across corporate changes the customer never agreed to.

What's market

12 months for direct employees is widely accepted in vendor contracts. The "interacted with" language is increasingly flagged as overbroad in standard contract review. The general-solicitation carve-out is essentially universal. Most pushback will come on duration; vendors prefer 18-24 months, customers prefer 6-12. Settle around 12.
