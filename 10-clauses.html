10 Clauses Solo Attorneys Most Often Miss

Ten patterns that slip past tired attorneys at 9pm on a Tuesday. For each one: the language to watch for, why it matters, what to redline, and what the other side will usually accept without much of a fight.

Why this list exists

I'm not a lawyer. I built a contract review tool, which means I spend a lot of time reading vendor agreements, SaaS terms, NDAs and employment offers looking at how they're drafted rather than who they're for. These ten are the patterns that come up again and again.

The dangerous language is almost never on the first page. It's tucked into section 14, or hidden behind a defined term you glossed past on page 3, or written in the kind of dense subordinate-clause sentence your eyes slide off at the end of a long day.

Two things worth saying up front. This is not legal advice and I am not your lawyer — use it as a checklist to sharpen your eye, not as a substitute for your own judgment about your client's situation. And the "what's market" notes are directional. They reflect common drafting practice, not a survey. Your jurisdiction, your industry and your specific deal may all push things in either direction.

1. Perpetual feedback license

Buried in the boilerplate of most SaaS agreements, this clause grants the vendor a permanent, irrevocable right to use any feedback your client provides — comments in support tickets, feature suggestions on a user research call, off-the-cuff remarks during a demo. The vendor can commercialize those ideas without paying your client anything.

Customer hereby grants to Provider a perpetual, irrevocable, worldwide, royalty-free license to use, modify, and incorporate into Provider's products any feedback, suggestions, or comments provided by Customer...

Why it matters. On its face this looks like polite legal hygiene. In practice it lets the vendor mine your client for product strategy, competitive intelligence and potentially patentable ideas, for free and forever. The uncomfortable version is a vendor taking a customer's specific workflow suggestions and building them into a product sold to that customer's competitors — with no recourse, because the customer licensed it away in boilerplate.

What to redline:

  • Strike "perpetual, irrevocable" and make the license terminate with the agreement.
  • Add a confidentiality carve-out so the vendor can't quote the client publicly or use them in case studies.
  • Limit "feedback" to product-functionality feedback, not anything that could be characterized as business intelligence or trade secrets.
  • Require consent before the vendor implements and markets a specific suggestion. Plenty of clients don't mind — nobody likes being surprised by it.

What's market. Most vendors will accept time-limiting the license to the term of the agreement. Confidentiality carve-outs are the harder push, because sophisticated vendors know feedback is valuable and will fight to keep it broadly licensed. Push anyway.

2. Auto-renewal with a restrictive cancellation window

The contract renews automatically for another term unless your client cancels within a specific window — often 60 or 90 days before the renewal date. Miss it by a day and you're in for another full year.

This Agreement shall automatically renew for successive one-year terms unless either party provides written notice of non-renewal at least ninety (90) days prior to the end of the then-current term.

Why it matters. These clauses are built to catch busy customers off guard. By the time anyone remembers the contract exists, they're already inside the no-cancellation zone. The worst versions pair a 90-day notice requirement with a window that only opens for 30 days — which gives the customer a month per year, scheduled at the vendor's convenience, to make the decision. That's not a contract. That's a trap with terms.

What to redline:

  • Reduce the notice period to 30 days at most.
  • Require the vendor to send a written renewal reminder 60 days before the deadline, as the vendor's obligation rather than the customer's tracking problem.
  • Add a one-time grace period: miss the window, get 30 more days to give notice without penalty.
  • If the vendor won't shorten the notice, change the renewal term instead — month-to-month rather than another full year.

What's market. 30-day notice is increasingly standard for SaaS. Several states have auto-renewal statutes requiring clear disclosure and easy cancellation, California's among them, but those apply primarily to consumer contracts — commercial customers are mostly on their own, so don't lean on them too hard in a B2B negotiation.

3. Unilateral price increases

The vendor can raise prices at any time, by any amount, with minimal notice. Sometimes the increase is capped. Often it isn't. Paired with auto-renewal, the vendor can raise prices 60 days before renewal and leave your client choosing between a 25% hike and a scramble to migrate.

Provider reserves the right to modify the fees set forth herein upon thirty (30) days' written notice, with such modifications taking effect on the first day of the following billing cycle.

Why it matters. Price increases become a hostage negotiation when migration costs are high. The vendor knows your client has built a workflow around the product, trained staff on it, and has data sitting inside it. They raise prices because they can, and the client pays because leaving in 30 days isn't realistic.

What to redline:

  • Cap annual increases at CPI or 5%, whichever is lower.
  • Require at least 90 days' notice of any increase.
  • Lock pricing for the initial term so increases can only land at renewal.
  • Add a termination right: if the vendor exceeds the cap, the customer can leave without penalty within 60 days of the notice.

What's market. CPI-capped increases are very standard. Many vendors will lock initial-term pricing without argument. The termination escape hatch is the harder ask, and it lands better framed as "we just want both sides still aligned at renewal" than as a threat.

4. One-sided indemnification

The customer agrees to indemnify the vendor against a long list of claims, while the vendor's reciprocal obligation is narrow or missing entirely. When something goes wrong, the customer pays regardless of who caused it.

Customer shall indemnify, defend, and hold harmless Provider and its affiliates from and against any and all claims, damages, losses, costs, and expenses (including reasonable attorneys' fees) arising out of or relating to Customer's use of the Service...

Why it matters. One-sided indemnification turns the customer into the vendor's insurance policy. If a vendor employee leaks customer data and a third party sues both companies, this clause can mean the customer pays the vendor's legal fees. These provisions tend to trigger in situations nobody pictured at signing.

What to redline:

  • Make it mutual — each party indemnifies the other for claims arising from its own conduct.
  • Carve out the vendor's gross negligence and willful misconduct. The customer should never indemnify the vendor for the vendor's own bad behavior.
  • Cap indemnification at the limitation-of-liability amount, or indemnity becomes an end-run around the liability cap.
  • Require the vendor to indemnify the customer for third-party IP claims arising from the vendor's own product.

What's market. Mutual indemnification is the norm. Vendor IP indemnification for its own product is standard. The carve-outs get negotiated, but one-way indemnity favoring the vendor is increasingly treated as a drafting red flag rather than a starting position.

5. Liability capped at the last six months of fees

The vendor's total liability is capped at whatever the customer paid in the preceding six months — sometimes three, sometimes twelve — regardless of how much damage the vendor causes.

In no event shall Provider's total cumulative liability exceed the fees paid by Customer to Provider in the six (6) months preceding the event giving rise to the claim.

Why it matters. Run the arithmetic. Your client pays $2,000 a month. The vendor has a breach that exposes 50,000 customer records. Between regulatory exposure, breach notification, reputational damage and potential litigation, the client is looking at seven figures. The vendor's maximum liability under this clause is $12,000. The client discovers at the worst possible moment that they never had meaningful recourse.

What to redline:

  • Move the cap to 12 months of fees, or 24.
  • Add a super cap for high-risk events — data breaches, IP infringement, gross negligence — at some multiple of annual fees.
  • Carve out direct damages from data breaches entirely where the data set is large or sensitive.
  • Make sure the cap doesn't swallow the vendor's indemnification obligations.

What's market. 12 months is increasingly standard. Super caps for data breaches and IP indemnification are routine in enterprise deals. Uncapped liability for breaches caused by vendor negligence is achievable for high-stakes customers, particularly if the client will pay a little more for it.

6. Data ownership left ambiguous

The contract is vague about who owns the data your client puts in, who owns derived or aggregated data, and what happens to model improvements trained on it. Ambiguity here almost always resolves in the vendor's favor.

Provider may use Customer Data in aggregate, anonymized form to improve the Service and develop new features. Provider owns all rights, title, and interest in the Service, including any improvements derived therefrom.

Why it matters. "Aggregate, anonymized" sounds harmless and isn't. Modern pipelines extract real value from aggregate behavioral data, and anonymization is a weaker standard than it sounds — re-identification research has repeatedly shown as much. Your client's data improves the vendor's model, the vendor gains a durable advantage, and your client gets nothing for it.

What to redline:

  • State customer ownership of customer data explicitly.
  • Restrict use of aggregate or derived data to operating the service — not product development, model training, or anything that creates new IP.
  • Add a deletion right: at termination, the vendor deletes customer data and derivatives within 30 days.
  • If the vendor insists on training rights, require opt-in and a corresponding discount. The data has value; price it.

What's market. Customer ownership of customer data is settled. The live fight is over derived data and model training. Many vendors will accept restrictions on training in exchange for keeping aggregate analytics, which is usually the trade worth making.

7. Choice of law and venue in a hostile jurisdiction

Disputes must be litigated under the vendor's preferred law, in the vendor's preferred court. Your client in Kansas City now has to travel to Delaware, hire Delaware counsel and litigate under Delaware law to enforce their own contract.

This Agreement shall be governed by and construed in accordance with the laws of the State of Delaware, without regard to its conflict of laws principles. Any dispute arising hereunder shall be subject to the exclusive jurisdiction of the state and federal courts located in Wilmington, Delaware.

Why it matters. Venue usually matters more than governing law. The cost of litigating somewhere distant is itself the deterrent — vendors know a client is unlikely to enforce anything if enforcement means out-of-state counsel. This is how a vendor quietly disclaims responsibility for everything else in the document.

What to redline:

  • Push for venue in your client's home jurisdiction. A neutral venue is the common compromise.
  • Decouple law from venue. Delaware law is fine; you don't have to be in Delaware to apply it.
  • Add a small-claims carve-out so claims under a set threshold can be brought anywhere competent.
  • If venue truly won't move, make the vendor cover reasonable travel costs for in-person proceedings.

What's market. Mid-market and smaller vendors will often negotiate venue. Enterprise vendors usually won't, but decoupling governing law from venue is almost always acceptable to them.

8. Arbitration with a class-action waiver

Disputes go to binding arbitration rather than court. The customer waives any right to participate in a class or representative action. Discovery is limited and appeals are restricted.

Any dispute arising out of or relating to this Agreement shall be resolved by binding arbitration under the rules of the American Arbitration Association. Customer waives any right to participate in a class action, collective action, or representative proceeding against Provider.

Why it matters. Arbitration sounds neutral and tends to favor the drafting party. Vendors pick providers they have history with. Limited discovery favors whoever holds more information, which is almost always the vendor. And class waivers stop customers pooling resources against small, widespread harms — precisely the harms a vendor is most likely to cause.

What to redline:

  • Strike the class-action waiver. This is the big one.
  • Require mutual agreement on the arbitrator rather than the vendor's unilateral choice.
  • Add a small-claims carve-out so claims under about $10,000 can go to court.
  • Make the prevailing party recover fees, which rebalances the asymmetric cost of arbitrating.

What's market. Be realistic here: in commercial contracts, class waivers are generally enforceable, and courts have been consistent about it. That's an argument for negotiating the clause rather than assuming it won't hold. Plenty of vendors will drop the waiver if pushed in a B2B deal, and small-claims carve-outs are common.

9. Overbroad non-solicitation

The customer agrees not to solicit any employee, contractor, or person "who interacts with" the vendor for a period after the contract ends. That last phrase can be read to capture the vendor's customers, partners and anyone in their orbit.

During the term of this Agreement and for a period of twenty-four (24) months thereafter, Customer shall not, directly or indirectly, solicit, recruit, or hire any employee, contractor, agent, or other individual who has interacted with Provider in connection with the Services.

Why it matters. These clauses existed to stop customers poaching vendor staff. Drafting has expanded well past that. Read literally, some would prevent hiring a freelancer the vendor used years ago, or contracting with another company whose founder once sat in on a sales call. Enforceability varies a great deal by state — some jurisdictions police overbreadth closely, others are far more permissive, and a few restrict these agreements heavily. Don't assume a clause is unenforceable just because it reads as excessive; few clients want to find out in litigation.

What to redline:

  • Narrow it to direct employees of the vendor, not anyone who "interacted with" them.
  • Reduce the duration to 12 months, ideally 6.
  • Add exceptions for general solicitation — job postings, recruitment ads — and for people who approach the customer first.
  • Terminate the non-solicit if the vendor is acquired or restructures.

What's market. 12 months for direct employees is widely accepted. The "interacted with" framing gets flagged routinely in contract review. A general-solicitation carve-out is close to universal.

10. Force majeure that excludes the likely scenarios

Force majeure excuses performance during extraordinary events. Modern drafting has evolved to specifically exclude the events most likely to actually occur — pandemics, supply-chain disruption, cyber incidents, labor action. The clause exists; it just doesn't cover anything you'd realistically need it for.

Force majeure events include acts of war, natural disasters, and government action, but expressly exclude pandemics, epidemics, supply-chain failures, cyber incidents, labor actions, and any event that was reasonably foreseeable at the time of contracting.

Why it matters. The clause is protection against the unforeseen, and carving out "anything reasonably foreseeable" guts it — in hindsight almost everything looks foreseeable. After 2020, vendors learned that broad force majeure language could let customers out of contracts, and the drafting response was to exclude pandemics specifically. Which is the one event the clause most obviously should cover.

What to redline:

  • Strike the specific exclusions for pandemics, supply-chain failures and cyber incidents.
  • Make the clause mutual. Both sides get the protection, not just the vendor.
  • Add a reasonable-mitigation requirement, without obliging either party to mitigate at unreasonable cost.
  • Cap the suspension: if performance is prevented for more than 90 days, the other party can terminate without penalty.

What's market. Mutual force majeure is standard. The post-2020 exclusions are increasingly negotiated back toward neutral. Termination rights after an extended suspension are common in anything running longer than a year.

A closing thought

You don't need to memorize these. Keep the headers in mind and skim for them — even a 30-second cross-check against this list catches most of the egregious stuff, which is more than the scan-sign-file treatment most contracts get.

These aren't the only clauses worth watching, either. Healthcare contracts have their own landmines, financial services carries regulatory exposure, government contracting is a universe unto itself. The patterns above generalize; the specifics will need adjusting for your work.

One rule of thumb. If a contract contains all ten of these, that isn't a contract — it's a hostage note dressed as a deal. Any one of them is negotiable. Ten at once is a vendor telling you they don't expect to be held to the agreement anyway.
