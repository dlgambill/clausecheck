What Is an Auto-Renewal Clause?

An auto-renewal clause causes the contract to renew automatically unless the customer cancels within a specific window. The shorter the window and the less notice the vendor provides, the more this clause functions as a trap rather than a normal commercial term.

What it does

Auto-renewal clauses make a contract renew automatically at the end of its initial term, typically for another full term of the same length. The customer can cancel — but only by giving written notice within a specific window before the renewal date.

The window is where the trap lives. A typical predatory clause requires 90 days' notice and only opens during the 30 days starting 120 days before renewal. That gives the customer a 30-day window per year, scheduled at the vendor's convenience, to make the renewal decision. Miss the window, and you're locked in for another year.

Consumer-protection laws have started addressing the worst versions of these clauses. California's Auto-Renewal Law requires clear disclosure of auto-renewal terms and easy cancellation methods. Several other states have similar laws. But these laws apply primarily to consumer contracts, not B2B; commercial customers are mostly on their own.

Why it matters

These clauses are designed to catch busy customers off guard. By the time you remember the contract exists, you're already inside the no-cancellation zone and stuck for another year. The worst versions pair a 90-day notice requirement with a window that opens only briefly, leaving the customer roughly 30 calendar days a year, scheduled at the vendor's convenience, to make the renewal decision. That's not a contract; that's a trap with terms. The vendor knows this. They draft it this way on purpose.

The language to watch for

Here's the kind of language you'll see in real contracts:

This Agreement shall automatically renew for successive one-year terms unless either party provides written notice of non-renewal at least ninety (90) days prior to the end of the then-current term.

What to redline

  • Reduce the cancellation notice period to 30 days at most. 90-day notices are increasingly rejected in standard contract review.
  • Require the vendor to send a written renewal reminder 60 days before the cancellation deadline — and make it the vendor's affirmative obligation, not the customer's tracking responsibility.
  • Add a one-time grace period: if the customer misses the cancellation window, they have an additional 30 days to give notice without penalty. This protects against good-faith oversight.
  • If the vendor refuses to shorten the notice period, change the renewal term itself. Make renewal month-to-month rather than another full year. The customer can leave with 30 days' notice after the initial term ends.
  • If the contract is governed by a state with auto-renewal consumer-protection laws (California, New York, others), invoke them — vendors usually fold once cited.

What's market

30-day notice periods are increasingly standard for SaaS and other recurring contracts. Renewal-reminder requirements are now legally mandated in some U.S. states. Cite the legal precedent in those jurisdictions, and the negotiation gets easier. Month-to-month auto-renewal (instead of annual) is a fallback position vendors will sometimes accept when they refuse to shorten the notice window.
