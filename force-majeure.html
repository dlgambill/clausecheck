What Is a Force Majeure Clause?

A force majeure clause excuses a party from contractual performance when extraordinary events make performance impossible. Post-2020, vendors have aggressively narrowed the scope of these clauses — sometimes to the point that they no longer cover the events most likely to actually happen.

What it does

Force majeure ("superior force") is a contractual concept dating to French civil law. The basic idea: if a true catastrophe makes performance impossible, the affected party shouldn't be in breach. Wars, natural disasters, government action — all typical triggers.

The clause usually does three things. First, it lists what counts as force majeure. Second, it specifies the consequences (typically: performance is suspended, neither party is in breach, the affected party gives notice). Third, it provides a termination right if force majeure persists for too long.

Modern drafting has dramatically narrowed what counts. Specifically, post-2020 force majeure clauses often exclude pandemics, supply-chain disruptions, cyber-attacks, and labor strikes — exactly the events most likely to actually happen in any given decade. The result is a clause that exists, but doesn't cover anything you'd realistically rely on it for.

Why it matters

Force majeure clauses are protection against the unforeseen. Carving out "anything reasonably foreseeable" essentially neuters the entire clause — by the time something happens, it's almost always reasonably foreseeable in retrospect. Post-2020, vendors learned that broad force majeure language could free customers from contracts during COVID. The industry response was to specifically exclude pandemics and similar events. But pandemics are exactly what force majeure is supposed to cover. The clause survives but does nothing.

The language to watch for

Here's the kind of language you'll see in real contracts:

Force majeure events include acts of war, natural disasters, and government action, but expressly exclude pandemics, epidemics, supply-chain failures, cyber incidents, labor actions, and any event that was reasonably foreseeable at the time of contracting.

What to redline

  • Strike specific exclusions for pandemics, supply-chain failures, and cyber incidents. These are the modern force majeure events; excluding them defeats the purpose.
  • Strike the "reasonably foreseeable" exception. This swallows the entire clause — by the time something has happened, it's almost always foreseeable in retrospect.
  • Make the clause mutual — both parties get force majeure protection, not just the vendor. One-sided force majeure is increasingly common and almost always inappropriate.
  • Add a reasonable-mitigation requirement: the affected party must take reasonable steps to mitigate, but not at unreasonable cost.
  • Cap the suspension period: if force majeure prevents performance for more than 90 days, the other party can terminate without penalty.

What's market

Mutual force majeure is standard. The specific-exclusion carve-outs added post-COVID are increasingly being negotiated back to neutral. Termination rights after extended suspension (typically 60-90 days) are common in any contract over 12 months. The harder fight is over including pandemics — vendors will resist, but the position is increasingly defensible.
